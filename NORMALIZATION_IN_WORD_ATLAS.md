# Normalization in Word Atlas

How raw counts of Bible words are made fair enough to compare, told for a reader who has never done it

Andrew Hopkins, with Claude. September 2026.

## A first introduction to normalization

Before turning to the Bible at all, it is worth seeing what normalization is in ordinary life, because every reader already does it without calling it by that name. Nothing in this section needs more mathematics than division. The rest of the essay builds on the ideas here, so a reader who takes these few pages slowly will find the later sections much easier.

### Comparing things of different sizes

Imagine two students. One scores 17 on a quiz; the other scores 38 on a test. Who did better? The question cannot be answered until one asks, "out of how many?" If the quiz had 20 questions and the test had 50, the first student answered 17 of 20, which is 85 percent, and the second answered 38 of 50, which is 76 percent. The larger raw number belonged to the weaker result. What made the comparison fair was dividing each score by the number of questions it was earned on, and then expressing both results on one common scale, "out of 100", which is what the word "percent" means.

That is normalization in its simplest form: a raw count is divided by the size of the thing it was counted in, so that counts taken from things of different sizes can be set side by side. The raw count answers "how many?" The normalized figure answers "how many, for its size?"

A second example makes the same point with places. A small town of 2,000 people has 3 public libraries; a city of 500,000 has 30. The city has ten times as many libraries, but it has two hundred and fifty times as many people. Divide each count by the population and multiply by 10,000 so the result is readable, and the town has 15 libraries for every 10,000 residents while the city has 0.6. The town is far better supplied. Newspapers do this constantly when they report something "per capita" or "per 100,000 residents"; they are normalizing.

### Why "per" a round number

Dividing a count by a size usually gives a small, awkward fraction. Three libraries divided by 2,000 people is 0.0015. Nobody can picture 0.0015, so the fraction is multiplied by a round number to make it readable: by 100 to make a percentage, by 10,000 to make "per 10,000 residents". The multiplying changes nothing about the comparison; it only moves the decimal point to where people can read it. Word Atlas multiplies by 1,000 and reports "per 1,000 words", which means: if this text were exactly a thousand words long and used the word at the same rate, this is how many times the word would appear.

A **share** is the same idea with a different divisor. Instead of dividing by the size of a place, one divides by a total of the same kind of thing. If a word occurs 100 times in the whole New Testament and 81 of those are in Acts, then Acts holds a share of 81 percent of that word. Rates and shares are the two everyday tools of normalization, and both appear throughout the atlas.

### Deciding what counts as the same thing

There is a second, earlier kind of normalization that happens before any counting, and it is easy to overlook. Suppose a clerk is asked to count how many times a man named William Carter appears in an old parish register. The register writes him as "William Carter", "Wm. Carter", "Will Carter" and "W. Carter", and sometimes spells the surname "Cartter". If the clerk counts only exact matches, one man becomes five, and none of the five counts is right. Before the clerk can count, a decision must be made about which written forms belong to one person. Making those decisions is also called normalization: many printed forms are gathered under one standard form so that they can be counted together.

Text has exactly this problem. The King James Bible prints "saith", "said", "sayest" and "saying", and a reader knows these are one verb. It prints "LORD" and "Lord", and a careful reader knows these render two different Hebrew words. A program knows none of this until it is told. So before Word Atlas counts anything, it applies a set of rules that decide which printed forms are to be counted as one word. The first half of this essay describes those rules.

### Beyond size: is the difference surprising?

Normalization makes two counts fair to compare, but it does not say whether a difference between them matters. Consider two coins. One is tossed 10 times and lands heads 7 times; the other is tossed 1,000 times and lands heads 700 times. Normalized, both show heads 70 percent of the time, exactly the same rate. Yet no one would judge them alike. Seven heads in ten tosses happens by pure chance with an ordinary coin roughly one time in six, so the first coin may be perfectly fair. Seven hundred heads in a thousand tosses essentially never happens by chance; the second coin is surely weighted.

The rates are identical; what differs is the amount of evidence behind them. Word Atlas faces this constantly, because a short chapter and a long book can show the same rate for a word while one of them offers far more evidence than the other. So after normalizing, the atlas takes one step further and asks how surprising a difference is, given how much text lies behind it. That measure is called **keyness**, and it is explained in its own section later. For now it is enough to hold on to the coins: the normalized rate says how strong a preference looks; keyness says how sure one can be that it is real.

### The trap of small numbers

One warning belongs in any introduction to normalization. Dividing by a small number magnifies whatever is divided. A hamlet of 50 people with a single library has 200 libraries per 10,000 residents, a figure that makes it look like the best-read place in the country when it has one small building. Normalized figures computed from very small texts behave the same way, and one stray occurrence in a short passage can look like a strong preference. Word Atlas guards against this by printing the raw count beside the rate wherever it can, and by marking figures that rest on too little text as "few". A good habit for every reader is to glance at the size behind a normalized figure before trusting it.

### Three questions

Everything in the rest of this essay can be held under three questions, asked in this order.

The first is: what counts as the same word? This is the gathering of spellings and forms under one root, the parish clerk's problem.

The second is: how much of it is there, for the size of the text? This is normalization in the narrow sense, the rates per 1,000 words and the shares, the students' scores and the town's libraries.

The third is: is that amount surprising, or could it be chance? This is keyness, the problem of the two coins.

The sections that follow take these questions one at a time, using the Bible text and the atlas's own pages for every example. Along the way they introduce the atlas's vocabulary, a set of plain words each with one fixed meaning, and its notation, a small set of marks used to write findings on one line. Each term is explained where it is first needed, and all of them are gathered again at the end.

## Why Word Atlas needs it

Every page of Word Atlas is built out of counts. The program counts how often a word occurs in a book, how often two words stand near each other, how often a phrase recurs, and how many rare words two verses share. Counting is easy. The difficulty is that a raw count, on its own, almost never means what it seems to mean. A word that occurs forty times in Psalms and forty times in Obadiah has the same count in both books, yet Psalms is roughly a hundred and fifty times longer than Obadiah, so forty occurrences in Obadiah is a flood and forty in Psalms is a trickle. Before any two counts can be set side by side, something has to be done to them so that the comparison is fair. That something is what this essay calls normalization.

As the introduction showed, the word is used in two related senses, and Word Atlas does both. The first sense concerns the words themselves: before anything can be counted, the program must decide which printed forms are "the same word". Is "saith" the same word as "said"? Is "LORD" the same word as "Lord"? Is "the heathen" the same phrase as "the nations"? Settling those questions is normalizing the text. The second sense concerns the numbers: once the words are counted, the counts are divided by the size of whatever they were counted in, so that a small book and a large book can stand on one scale. Word Atlas's own vocabulary uses the word in this second, narrower sense, and defines it as dividing a raw count by the size of the thing it was counted in. This essay covers both, in the order the program applies them, because the second cannot be understood without the first. It then goes one step further, as the program does, to the measure called keyness, which asks not only how much of a word there is for its size but whether that amount is surprising.

No experience with statistics or with computer programs is assumed. Every term is introduced where it is first needed and illustrated with an example from the Bible text. The notation the atlas uses to write its findings is introduced in its own section, one mark at a time, and each mark is shown at work. By the end the reader should be able to look at any line on any page of the atlas and say what was counted, what it was divided by, and what the resulting number means.

## The picture behind the program

Word Atlas takes its name from a road atlas. On a map of the world one sees the relations between continents. Turn the page to a map of one country and a different set of relations appears, invisible at the world scale. Turn again to a map of one state and a third set appears. Words behave in the same way. The word "LORD" casts a large shadow over the whole Bible. The word "vine" casts almost none at that scale, yet in John 15 it is the largest thing on the page. The whole purpose of the program is to let a reader turn from one scale to another and watch how a word's importance changes.

That purpose is what makes normalization unavoidable. The instant one says that "vine" matters more in John 15 than in the Bible as a whole, one is comparing a count taken in a chapter of a few hundred words with a count taken in a text of nearly eight hundred thousand. The raw counts cannot be compared at all. "Vine" occurs more times in the whole Bible than in John 15, simply because the whole Bible contains John 15 and a great deal besides. Only when each count is set against the size of the text it came from does the chapter's preference for the word become visible. Normalization is the step that turns "how many" into "how many, for its size", and without it the atlas could not turn a single page.

## The first vocabulary: text, token, spelling and root

A few plain words are needed before anything else can be described, and the atlas uses each of them in one fixed sense.

A **scale** is the amount of text being looked at: the whole Bible, one testament, one book, one section of a book, one chapter, or a passage of a few verses. Everything in the atlas is measured at some scale, and the same word can have very different measurements at different scales. When the atlas writes about a scale it puts it in square brackets, so that `[Joel]` means "at the scale of the book of Joel" and `[Bible]` means "across the whole Bible". This notation will be explained fully later; it is mentioned now only so that the brackets are familiar when they appear.

A **token** is one word in one place. The King James text of Genesis 1:1 reads "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth." That verse holds ten tokens. The third of them is the token "beginning", and it is a different token from any other "beginning" anywhere else in the Bible, because it stands in a different place. The whole atlas holds about 790,000 tokens. When the program counts, it is counting tokens.

A **spelling** is the form a token takes as the text prints it. "Day", "days" and "day's" are three spellings. "Saith", "said", "sayest" and "spake" are spellings too, though of two different verbs.

A **root** is what a token is counted as. This is the heart of the first kind of normalization. If the program counted spellings, then "day" and "days" would be two different words, each with half the count they ought to share, and the importance of the idea of a day would be split in two. So each token is assigned a root, and every token with the same root is counted as the same word. The spelling is what the reader sees; the root is what the program counts.

Word Atlas has two ways of deciding a root, and it can be built either way. The first uses English stems. A **stem** is the English base of a word with its endings taken off: "day" for "days", "come" for "cometh" and "coming", "say" for "saith" and "said". The second, which is now the default, uses Strong's numbers. The King James Bible has been tagged, word by word, with the numbers of James Strong's concordance, in which every Hebrew word of the Old Testament and every Greek word of the New has its own number. A number beginning with H is Hebrew and one beginning with G is Greek. The number the tagging attaches to a token is called its **tag**. Under this second method a token's root is the Hebrew or Greek word behind it, named by its number. So the divine name YHWH, which the King James prints as "LORD", is H3068. The Hebrew title Adonai, which it prints as "Lord", is H136. The Greek kurios, also printed "Lord", is G2962. These are three different roots even though two of them are spelled identically in English. In the other direction, the Greek word G2112 is printed sometimes as "straightway" and sometimes as "immediately", and under Strong's roots those two English words are one root.

When the atlas prints a root under this method it prints the English spelling followed by the number, as in "lord H3068" or "left G863". The English part is only a label, chosen as the commonest spelling of that root in the scope being looked at; the number is the root itself.

A **stop word** is a function word such as "the", "of", "and", "shall", "unto" or "hath". These words are everywhere and attach to nothing in particular, so they are left out when the program counts words, neighbors and kin. They are, however, kept inside phrases, because "the day of the LORD" would be unrecognizable as "day LORD". The full set of stop words is called the **stop list**. The stop list has one firm rule written into it from the start: it never includes "LORD", "God" or "Lord", however common they are, because in the Bible those are content words of the first importance.

A **content word** is simply any word that is not a stop word. Most of what the atlas measures is measured on content words.

Finally, **weight** is the atlas's plain word for a count. The weight of a word at a scale is how many times it occurs there. (The word is also used, in a related sense, for echoes, where it means summed rarity; that sense will be explained when rarity is.)

## The first normalization: deciding what counts as the same word

Before a single count is divided by anything, every token in the Bible passes through a series of steps that decide its root. Each step is a small act of normalization, a rule that takes many printed forms and gathers them under one. It is worth walking through them in the order the program applies them, because a surprising amount of what the pages later show depends on these quiet decisions.

The first step is to straighten punctuation inside words. The database that holds the King James text uses a curly apostrophe in places. In the earliest Joel report a stray word "s" appeared among the echoes, and it turned out that a curly apostrophe had split "Esau's" into "Esau" and "s". Now apostrophes are straightened before words are split, and when a word is compared with the tagging the apostrophes and hyphens are removed altogether, since the tagging spells "Beth-el" as "Bethel" and "king's" as "kings". This is normalization at its most mechanical: two printings of one word are made identical so that the computer does not mistake them for two.

The second step is the treatment of capitals. Nearly every word is lowered to small letters, so that "And" at the start of a verse and "and" in the middle are one word. But the King James prints the divine name in small capitals, as "LORD" and "GOD", to show that the Hebrew behind it is YHWH rather than Adonai or Elohim. Lowering those would merge the divine name with the title. So a short list of divine names, LORD, GOD, JEHOVAH and JAH, keep their capitals as part of their identity, and every other capitalized word is lowered. The rule once kept every all-capital word as its own token, but that made the inscription on the cross, "THE KING OF THE JEWS", look like a rare phrase found in only a few places, and it headed every echo list. Narrowing the rule to the divine names removed the error. This is a good example of how a normalization rule is judged: by whether the pages it produces say something true about the text.

The third step, on a Strong's build, is to place each Strong's tag on its token. In the New Testament the tagging and the King James words line up one for one. In the Old Testament they drift, because the tagged source counts an untranslated Hebrew particle (H853, the marker of a direct object, which English does not render) and the headings of the Psalms as words. So the program places each tag by looking for its word near where its position says it should be, allowing for the drift found so far in that verse. About 99.7 percent of the tags land. Where a word has been given two numbers, as "shoes" is given G846 and G5266 (G846 being "his"), the rarer number is taken as the root, since the common numbers are the grammatical ones.

The fourth step deals with words the tagger left bare. The tagger gives one number to one English word, and where one Hebrew or Greek word became two or three English words, the others are left without a number. Word Atlas has three rules for such words, and each is a normalization in its own right.

The first of these is called the gloss rule. Strong's dictionary is loaded into the atlas as the **lexicon**, and for every number it gives the English meanings the King James uses for it, called its **glosses**. When a bare word stands within two stop words of a tagged word whose gloss names it, the bare word is folded into that tagged word. In "sick of the palsy" the Greek word paralytikos (G3885) is tagged on "palsy", and "sick" is bare. The dictionary's gloss for G3885 names "sick of the palsy", so "sick" is folded into G3885. In another verse of Mark, where "sick" belongs to a different Greek word, the same rule sends it there instead, because the rule decides each case on the dictionary's evidence and not by a blanket substitution. On the last rebuild this rule folded about 14,580 words: "burnt offering", "round about", "went out", "cut off", "young men", "fine linen", "came to pass", and many more.

The second is absorption by company. An untagged word that stands beside the same tagged word at least sixty percent of the time, and does so at least five times in its testament, is folded into that word. "Chief" is folded into "priests" (G749), "burnt" into "offering" (H5930), "thus" into "saith" (H559), "right" into "hand" (H3225). These are the King James's two-word renderings of a single Hebrew or Greek word.

A token folded in either of these two ways is said to be **absorbed**, and the atlas marks it with an equals sign before the number it joined, as "=G749". An absorbed token is then set aside like a stop word, so that the Greek word for "chief priests" is counted once and not twice.

The third rule is inference. A bare word that is not absorbed may take the number its spelling usually carries, first within the same book and then within the same testament, but only when tagging is the norm for that spelling: it must be tagged at least three times there, one number must hold at least half of those tags, and its tagged occurrences must outnumber its untagged ones. A token given its number in this way is said to be **inferred**, and it is marked with a tilde before the number, as "~G5207". The supplied "son" in Luke's genealogy, which the tagger left bare because there is no Greek word behind it, is inferred as G5207, the Greek for son. A token that received its number directly from the tagging, by contrast, is said to be **placed**. So every tagged token in the atlas is placed, inferred or absorbed, and the marks tell which.

A word that passes through all of these rules without receiving a number keeps its English stem as its root. After all the rules have run, about 3.4 percent of the content words are in that state, and nearly all of them are words the text really does divide between two Hebrew or Greek roots ("went" between H3212 and H1980, for example), where a guess would do more harm than a gap.

The fifth step, for any word that ends up with an English root, is the stemmer. The stemmer is a small set of rules that removes English endings: "-eth", "-est", "-ing", "-ed", "-es" and "-s", tried in order, with a few safeguards so that "pass", "darkness" and "jealous" keep their final letters. The King James has many archaic forms no ending rule can handle, so the stemmer begins with a table of exceptions: "saith", "said" and "sayest" all go to "say"; "spake" and "spoken" to "speak"; "hath", "hast" and "had" to "have"; "children" to "child"; "leaves" to "leaf". The table grew by reading. The first pages showed "hundred" counted as "hundr" and "counsel" as "counsell", and "leaves" had been folded into "leave" rather than "leaf". Each such fault was an exception added to the table. Because a stem is not always a real word, the atlas never prints stems; it prints the commonest real spelling of the root instead.

The sixth step is the stop list, which marks each token as a stop word or a content word. The stop list was enlarged when a count of the bare words in the Strong's build found that more than half of them were forty-odd English grammar words (hath, shalt, hast, against, therefore, thereof, because, mine, himself) that Hebrew and Greek carry as prefixes or endings rather than as separate words. The tagger had rightly left them alone, but the atlas had been counting them as content. They went on the stop list.

When all six steps have run, every token in the Bible has a spelling, a root, and a mark saying whether it is a stop word. That is the text normalized. Everything that follows is counted on roots, so that when the atlas reports that a word occurs twenty-four times in Malachi, it means twenty-four tokens whose root is that word, whatever their spellings.

One more kind of text normalization belongs here, because it applies to phrases rather than words. A **formula** is a set phrase of two to five words that recurs, such as "the day of the LORD" or "and it came to pass". On a Strong's build a formula is not stored as English words but as a run of **units**: each tagged content word stands as its Strong's number, and each stop word or untagged word stands as its spelling. So the phrase "the heathen" and the phrase "the nations", which render the same Hebrew word H1471, are one formula, found in 323 verses of 33 books. "A ram without blemish", "rams without blemish" and "ram without blemish" are one formula without any special folding. The atlas then displays each formula in its commonest English wording, read off the verses that hold it, so the reader sees familiar English while the counting is done on the Hebrew and Greek.

## The notation

Word Atlas writes its findings in a small notation of marks, each with one fixed meaning. The same marks appear in the section titles of the pages, in the headings of the verse pane, in the Ask line of the window where a reader can type them to open a page, and in conversation about the findings. The notation was kept deliberately small, and the period and comma keep their ordinary jobs. Several of the marks have already appeared; here they are all, in turn.

Double quotation marks enclose a formula or an echo in the exact words of the text. `"the day of the LORD"` means that phrase as the King James prints it. Typed into the Ask line, it opens every verse that holds the phrase.

Single quotation marks enclose a word as the atlas counts it, that is, a root with all its spellings folded together. `'say'` means every token whose root is "say", which includes "saith", "said" and "sayest". This is the mark that shows the first kind of normalization at work: the reader writes one word in single quotes and means every spelling gathered under it. A Strong's number in single quotes names a root outright, so `'H3068'` is the divine name however the text spells it.

Square brackets enclose the scale being looked at. `'day' [Joel]` is the word "day" at the scale of Joel; `'day' [Joel 2]` is the same word in the second chapter of Joel; `'day' [Bible]` is the word across the whole Bible. `[Old]` and `[New]` name the testaments, and `[Psalms: Book II]` names one section of a book.

A minus sign inside square brackets takes one scale out of another. `[Bible - Revelation]` means the whole Bible with Revelation removed, which is to say the rest of the Bible. This mark is more important to normalization than it may look, because it names the comparison text. When the atlas asks whether a word is unusually common in Revelation, it does not compare Revelation with the whole Bible, since the whole Bible contains Revelation and the comparison would be partly with itself. Revelation holds thirty-two of the Bible's seventy-two occurrences of "seal", nearly half, so comparing Revelation with a Bible that includes Revelation would hide much of the difference. It compares Revelation with `[Bible - Revelation]` instead.

Parentheses enclose a measurement, named, on whatever stands before it. `'hosts' [Malachi] (weight 24, keyness 126.8)` says that the word "hosts", counted at the scale of Malachi, has a weight of 24 and a keyness of 126.8. Keyness is explained below; for now it is enough to see how the mark is read: the thing measured, the scale, and in parentheses the measurements with their names.

Braces enclose a set of words in which order does not matter. `{river, tree, fruit, leaves, month}` is the set of rare words that Ezekiel 47:12 and Revelation 22:2 share. Braces are used for kin, which is explained below.

Angle brackets enclose words in a fixed order. `<river, tree, fruit, month>` says that those four words occur in that order in both verses, with anything at all allowed between them.

A plus sign joins two words that meet inside the window, that is, within five words of each other in the same verse. `'day' + 'night' [Ezekiel] (5 meetings)` says that "day" and "night" meet five times within the window in Ezekiel. Two words joined this way are called a neighbor pair.

A lower-case x sets two books against each other, chapter by chapter. `[Exodus] x [Leviticus]` names the Compare page for those two books.

An arrow marks an echo or kin link from one place to another. `"the days of your fathers" Joel 1:2 -> Malachi 3:7` says that the phrase occurs in Joel 1:2 and again in Malachi 3:7. Typed into the Ask line with a question mark after it, as `Ezekiel 47 -> ?`, the arrow asks for the passages kin to Ezekiel 47.

An equals sign joins spellings folded to one root. `saith = said = 'say'` says that the two spellings are both counted as the root "say". This is the first kind of normalization written down in one line. (The same sign, placed directly before a Strong's number as in "=G749", marks an absorbed token, as described earlier; the meaning is the same idea, a form gathered into a root, seen from the token's side.)

With these marks a whole finding fits on one line. The atlas's own model sentence is this: `'day' + 'gloominess' [Joel] (pull 14.1)` against `'day' + 'month' [Bible - Joel] (pull 497)`. Read aloud, it says: the word "day", counted with all its spellings, meets the word "gloominess" within the window in the book of Joel, and the strength of that tie is 14.1; set that beside the tie between "day" and "month" in the rest of the Bible, whose strength is 497. The contrast is the finding. Across the Bible, "day" keeps the company of the calendar (months, sabbaths, feasts); in Joel it keeps the company of darkness and gloom. A second model sentence shows the other marks: `Ezekiel 47:12 -> Revelation 22:2 {river, tree, fruit, leaves, month}, <river, tree, fruit, month> in order, 4 of 5`. Ezekiel 47:12 is linked to Revelation 22:2 by five shared rare words, and four of the five come in the same order in both verses.

## The second normalization: putting counts on one scale

With the text normalized, the program can count. It counts the weight of every root at every scale: in the Bible, in each testament, in each book, in each chapter. And at once the problem described at the start of this essay appears. The counts are not comparable, because the texts they were counted in are of very different sizes. The book of Psalms has about 43,000 words; Obadiah has fewer than 700; Genesis about 38,000; Philemon about 450. A count of ten means something entirely different in each.

The remedy is to divide each count by the size of the text it came from. This is normalization in the atlas's own narrow sense: dividing a raw count by the size of the thing it was counted in, so that parts of different sizes can be compared. Dividing by the size gives a very small fraction, a proportion of all the words, which is awkward to read, so the atlas multiplies it by a thousand and reports the figure **per 1,000 words**. The phrase means: if this text were exactly a thousand words long and the word were used at the same rate, this is how many times it would occur.

A simple example with round numbers shows the arithmetic. Suppose a book of 10,000 words contains a certain word 20 times. Divide 20 by 10,000 and the result is 0.002, which is to say two thousandths of the book's words are this word. Multiply by 1,000 and the rate is 2.0 per 1,000 words. Now suppose the rest of the Bible, some 90,000 words in this imaginary example, contains the same word 40 times. That is twice the raw count, and a careless reading would say the word matters more in the rest of the Bible than in the book. But 40 divided by 90,000 and multiplied by 1,000 is about 0.44 per 1,000 words. For its size, the book uses the word four and a half times as often as the rest of the text. The raw counts pointed one way; the normalized rates point the other, and the normalized rates are the true account of preference.

A real example from the atlas is the word "selah". It occurs 71 times in the Psalter's roughly 43,000 words, and only 3 times in the rest of the Old Testament's roughly 560,000 words. Normalized, that is about 1.65 per 1,000 words in the Psalms against about 0.005 per 1,000 words in the rest of the Old Testament: more than three hundred times the rate. In this case the raw counts already told the story, but the normalized figures say by how much, and they would say it just as well if the two texts were the same size.

The per 1,000 figure appears in many places in the atlas. The signature words table of a book page (section 1) gives each word's rate per 1,000 words in the book beside its rate in the comparison text, so the reader can see the difference directly. The shadow map on a word page draws one bar for each of the sixty-six books, and the length of each bar is the word's rate per 1,000 words in that book, not its raw count, because a raw count would make the long books tower over the short ones whatever the word. With the rates, "day" runs highest in Obadiah, Zephaniah, Haggai and Esther, small books where the day is the subject, and lowest in the letters. The partner table (section 4a) reports each echo partner's echoes per 1,000 words of the partner, so that Leviticus, a mid-sized book, can be seen to be a denser source for Ezekiel than Jeremiah, a long one, even though Jeremiah shares more echoes by raw count. The section maps (7a and 7b) sum their echoes to sections of a book and divide by the sections' sizes, because Book V of the Psalter has about 12,500 words and Book III about 6,800, and on raw echo weight the larger part would win simply by being larger.

The same operation with a different divisor is a **share**. Where a rate divides a count by the size of a text, a share divides a count by a total of the same thing. The home map on the testament page shows, for each book and word, the share of the word's occurrences in the testament that fall in that book. The name "Paul" has 81 percent of its New Testament occurrences in Acts; the Greek word for "returned" (G5290) has 59 percent in Luke. A dark cell on that map is a word that lives in one book, and a column of pale cells is a word spread across the testament. Shares are also used as guards. The sharing table (section 4d) passes over a partner book when more than 80 percent of its echo weight falls in five chapters of the book being read, because such a partner is really one passage borrowed, not a steady source: 2 Kings, with 94 percent of its echo weight on Isaiah falling in Isaiah 7 and 36 to 39, gives way to broader partners, while Daniel, with 59 percent across Revelation, keeps its place.

### Choosing what to divide by

Every normalization depends on a choice of divisor, and much of the care in Word Atlas has gone into choosing it well. Three choices deserve explanation, because each changed what the pages said.

The first is the choice of comparison text, already met in the notation as `[Bible - Revelation]`. A book is compared with the rest of the Bible, never with a Bible that includes it. When a book holds a large share of a word, the difference matters a great deal, as the seals of Revelation show.

The second is the choice of testament. When the atlas moved to Strong's numbers, the first signature lists for the Gospels were full of ordinary Greek verbs. The reason was a problem of divisor. A Greek number can occur only in the twenty-seven books of the New Testament, and the New Testament is only about a quarter of the Bible. Compared with the whole Bible, every Greek word looks four times more common in any Gospel than "expected", because the three quarters of the Bible written in Hebrew cannot contain it at all. Matthew's list described Koine Greek rather than Matthew. The cure was to compare a Strong's number with the rest of its own testament: a Hebrew root with the rest of the Old Testament, a Greek root with the rest of the New. An English stem, which can occur in either testament, is still compared with the rest of the Bible. With that one change the Gospel lists became the characteristic vocabulary of each evangelist that scholars had compiled by hand a century before, which was the strongest confirmation the atlas has had that its rules were right. The column headings on the pages say which comparison is being made.

The third is the choice of what counts as one occurrence. In the Malachi report a phrase repeated three times inside a single verse scored as highly as a phrase found in three different verses spread through the book. That ran against the original idea of the atlas, that many repeats in a small space should count for less than the same number spread over a wide area. So a formula's keyness is now judged on the number of different verses it occurs in, with the raw count shown beside it. This is sometimes called the small-space rule. It is a normalization of repetition: the unit of counting is changed from the token to the verse, so that a refrain packed into one sentence does not pass itself off as a habit of the whole book.

### Observed against expected

One more figure belongs to this family, and it appears in the partner table as **obs/exp**, short for observed against expected. For each partner book the atlas counts the echoes the two books actually share, which is the observed figure. It then works out how many echoes the partner would be expected to share if echoes simply fell in proportion to the partner's length, which is the expected figure. The ratio of the first to the second says how far the partner exceeds what its size alone would predict. A ratio of one means the partner shares exactly what its length would suggest; two means twice that; below one means less. For Revelation, Daniel runs at about four and a half times expectation, and because the figure has been normalized for length it can be compared with the same figure for any other pair of books. Obs/exp is therefore normalization turned into a single number with a natural meaning: above one is more than chance would give, below one is less.

## Beyond normalization: keyness

Normalization answers the question "how much, for its size?" It does not answer the question "is that amount remarkable?" The difference matters. A word that occurs twice in a 200-word chapter has a rate of 10 per 1,000, which might be far above its rate elsewhere, yet two occurrences in a short chapter could easily happen by chance. A word with the same rate in a 20,000-word book, occurring two hundred times, is another matter altogether. The rates are identical; the confidence one can have in them is not. Word Atlas therefore goes one step beyond normalization to a measure called **keyness**, which asks how surprising a word's count is, given its count in the comparison text and the sizes of both.

Keyness is computed by a standard test called the **log-likelihood**, and the particular form the atlas uses is **Dunning's G squared**, named after Ted Dunning, whose 1993 paper on "the statistics of surprise" introduced it to the study of language. Corpus linguists have used it to find the key words of a text ever since. The name sounds forbidding, but the idea can be followed step by step with nothing more than the arithmetic of the last section and one extra operation, the logarithm, which the reader may take as a device that turns ratios into sums.

The procedure has four steps. First, take the word's count in the text of interest and in the comparison text, and the sizes of the two texts. Second, work out what each count would have been if the word had been spread over both texts evenly, in proportion to their sizes. These are the **expected counts**. Third, for each count, divide the observed count by the expected count, take the logarithm of that ratio, and multiply by the observed count. Fourth, add the results and double the sum. If the word is rarer in the text of interest than expected, the sign is made negative, so that a negative keyness means "less than expected here".

Return to the imaginary example: 20 occurrences in a 10,000-word book and 40 in a 90,000-word comparison text. Together the two texts hold 100,000 words and 60 occurrences. Spread evenly in proportion to size, the book, which is a tenth of the total, would hold a tenth of the 60, which is 6, and the comparison text would hold the other 54. Those are the expected counts. Now the observed counts are set against them. In the book the word was observed 20 times against an expected 6, a ratio of about 3.33; its logarithm is about 1.20, and multiplied by 20 that gives about 24.1. In the comparison text the word was observed 40 times against an expected 54, a ratio of about 0.74; its logarithm is about minus 0.30, and multiplied by 40 that gives about minus 12.0. Add the two, about 12.1, and double: the keyness is about 24.2.

Dunning's full formula has four cells rather than two. Besides the word here and the word there, it also counts all the other words here and all the other words there, and does the same arithmetic on them. The README describes all four. On texts the size of Bible books the two extra cells cancel almost exactly (in the example above they add about 0.02), so the atlas's code computes the two cells for the word itself and the result is the same to the first decimal place. For selah the two-cell figure is 350.3 and the four-cell figure 350.4.

That selah figure is worth following through, because it is the example the project keeps. Selah occurs 71 times in the Psalter's 43,000 words and 3 times in the rest of the Old Testament's 560,000. Together that is 74 occurrences in 603,000 words. Spread evenly, the Psalter, being about 7 percent of the whole, would hold about 5.3 of the 74, and the rest of the Old Testament about 68.7. The Psalter's observed 71 against its expected 5.3 is a ratio of about 13.5, whose logarithm is about 2.60; times 71 is about 184.5. The rest's observed 3 against its expected 68.7 is a ratio of about 0.044, whose logarithm is about minus 3.13; times 3 is about minus 9.4. The sum is about 175.1, and doubled it is about 350. The atlas calls selah the Psalter's word, and means it.

What does a keyness of 350 mean, or 24, or 3? The log-likelihood has well-known thresholds. A keyness above 3.8 means the difference is unlikely to be chance, with only one chance in twenty that a difference so large would appear if the word were really spread evenly. Above 6.6 the odds are one in a hundred. Above 10.8 they are one in a thousand. A keyness near zero means the word is spread evenly and there is nothing to see. A large negative keyness means the word is conspicuously absent. So selah at 350 is far beyond any doubt, and the imaginary word at 24 is also well beyond chance. When the notation writes `'hosts' [Malachi] (weight 24, keyness 126.8)`, it says that "hosts" occurs 24 times in Malachi and that this is so far above what the rest of the Bible would predict that it could not plausibly be chance. Malachi's "saith the LORD of hosts" stands in 21 of its 55 verses, and the keyness is the counting's way of noticing it.

The reason for choosing Dunning's test over the older chi-squared test, which does a similar job, is that chi-squared exaggerates for rare words and small texts, and the Bible is full of both. A word that occurs three times in a short prophet would be flagged as wildly significant by chi-squared; the log-likelihood stays honest.

### What keyness is not

One property of keyness must be understood before the pages can be read correctly, and it is the reason the atlas keeps normalized rates beside it. Keyness grows with the size of the evidence. Take the imaginary example and shrink it by ten: 2 occurrences in a 1,000-word text against 4 in a 9,000-word text. The rates per 1,000 words are exactly what they were, 2.0 against 0.44. But the keyness falls to about 2.4, below the threshold of 3.8, which is to say that with so little text a difference of that size could easily be chance. Now enlarge the example by ten: 200 in 100,000 against 400 in 900,000. The rates are still 2.0 and 0.44, and the keyness rises to about 241.

So keyness is not a normalized figure. It measures confidence, how sure one can be that a difference is real, and confidence rises with the amount of evidence. Normalized rates measure intensity, how strongly a text prefers a word, regardless of how much text there is. The two answer different questions, and a reader who confuses them will be misled. A long book will generally post higher keyness figures than a short book for the same intensity of preference, simply because it offers more evidence. That is why the signature words table prints both the rate per 1,000 words and the keyness, and why, when the atlas compares sections of different sizes in the reach-and-depth chart with the section as its unit (7c), it prints "depth/1000" beside depth: the same keyness divided by the section's size in thousands of words, which removes the advantage of size. On that measure the divine title Adonai YHWH is deepest by keyness in Ezekiel 1 to 24, the longest part, but deepest for its length in the restoration chapters, while the temple vision's cubits are deepest on both.

## Rarity

A third kind of number runs through the atlas beside normalized rates and keyness. **Rarity** says how uncommon a word is anywhere, without reference to any particular book. It is computed as the negative logarithm of the word's share of all the words in its testament. The share is a normalization (a count divided by a total), and the logarithm turns it into a convenient scale where rarer words score higher. A word that is one in a thousand of its testament's words scores about 6.9, usually rounded to 7. A word that is one in two thousand scores about 7.6. A word that is one in a hundred thousand scores about 11.5. Each time a word becomes ten times rarer, its rarity rises by about 2.3. The logarithm is used so that rarities can be added: the rarity of a phrase is the sum of the rarities of its content words, and that sum behaves sensibly where a product of tiny fractions would not.

Rarity is what gives an echo its weight, and this is the second sense of the word **weight** promised earlier. An **echo** is a formula of three or more words that occurs in the book being read and in another book, and is rare enough to mean something: it may occur in no more than six verses of the whole Bible, or it is idiom rather than echo. The weight of an echo is the summed rarity of its content words. So "ten thousand times ten thousand", shared by Revelation 5 and Daniel 7, outweighs "a voice from heaven saying", because its words are rarer, even though both are three or more words long. A single striking shared sentence therefore counts for more than a dozen shared commonplaces. When the partner table is sorted by weight rather than by raw count, bland idiom stops outvoting quotation. The Compare page draws a floor under its chapter lists at a weight of 35, which two rare phrases or one very rare phrase will clear and a pair of commonplace ones will not.

Rarity also sets the threshold for **kin**. Two verses are kin when they share three or more rare words in any order, rare meaning one in two thousand or rarer, a rarity of about 7.6 or more. The score of a kin pair is the summed rarity of the words they share, raised by up to half again when the shared words come in the same order in both verses. Kin was invented because some dependence runs through imagery rather than wording. John paraphrases Ezekiel rather than quoting him, so Ezekiel 47 and Revelation 22 share river, tree, fruit, leaves and healing and not one formula. The notation writes the link as `Ezekiel 47:12 -> Revelation 22:2 {river, tree, fruit, leaves, month}`, and the braces say that order was not required. Kin was first tried on whole chapters as bags of words, and long chapters won every time because they hold more words: a failure of normalization, since the chapter's size was driving the score. The rule was changed to compare verse with verse, which puts every comparison on a small and roughly equal footing.

The highest grade of echo is called **quotation grade**: an echo of five or more words found in exactly two verses of the whole Bible, one in each book. Its test combines rarity and count, and it is the strongest evidence the atlas offers that one text read another.

## Measures built on the normalized counts

The remaining measures of the atlas are all built on these three foundations, and each can now be described in a sentence or two.

**Signature words** are the words far more common at a scale than in the rest of its testament or Bible, that is, the words with the highest keyness there. Joel's signature words on the first report were cankerworm, zion, pasture, wine, palmerworm, locust, desolate, heathen, valley, howl, wither and rain: the book in a dozen words.

**Reach** is how widely a word is spread across the current scale: in how many books of the Bible, or how many chapters of a book, it appears. It is the horizontal dimension of a word's presence. **Spread** combines reach with keyness: it is the word's keyness multiplied by the share of the book's chapters the word reaches, which is another normalization, reach divided by the number of chapters available. A word that lives in under a fifth of a book's chapters is marked **local**, a book within the book. In Ezekiel, the Hebrew word for "side" (H6285) has a keyness of 359 but reaches only 5 of the 48 chapters, so its spread by that rule is only about 37 and it is marked local; it belongs to the temple vision. The divine title "god" (H3069, the GOD of "the Lord GOD") has a keyness of 834 and reaches 42 of the 48 chapters, so its spread stays high: it is the voice of the whole book.

**Depth** is the vertical dimension: how thickly a word piles up in one small place. It is measured as the highest keyness the word reaches in any single chapter, and the chapter where it does so is recorded as the word's deepest chapter. The same Ezekiel pair shows the two dimensions pulling apart. "God" H3069, spread through the book, has a depth of only 69; "side" H6285, piled up in chapter 48, has a depth of 314. Depth draws on an old observation of biblical scholarship, the Leitwort or **leading word** of Buber and Rosenzweig, a word deliberately repeated within a short stretch so that the repetition carries the meaning. The atlas uses "leading words" for the words whose deepest chapter in the whole book is the chapter being read: the leading words of Ezekiel 40 are cubits, gate, arches, measured and breadth. The **reach-and-depth chart** plots every signature word with reach across and depth up, so that the book's leading words sit at top right, its spread words at bottom right, and its local piles at top left.

**Neighbors** are the words that fall within the **window** of a given word more often than chance predicts. The window is five words either side, never crossing the end of a verse. It was chosen to be wide enough to catch "the day of the LORD is near" as neighbors of "day" and narrow enough that a long verse does not become one undifferentiated cloud. One pairing of a word with a neighbor that meets it more often than chance is a **tie**, and the strength of that tie is its **pull**. Pull is a cousin of keyness applied to pairs: it asks how much more often than chance the two words meet within the window, given how common each is. A word's **shadow** is its ties added up, the summed pull of every neighbor it draws more often than chance. This is the idea the whole project began from: "LORD" casts a large shadow because it is tied strongly to many words; "a" casts almost none, because though it is everywhere it attracts nothing in particular. The neighbors table on a book page sets the book's neighbors beside the rest of the Bible's, `[Bible - Joel]` beside `[Joel]`, so the comparison is never with a text that contains the book itself.

**Home** is the book that prefers a word most, by keyness against the rest of its testament, and a book's home words are the words whose home it is. The testament page lists six home words for each book. Hebrews has covenant, offered, better, sacrifice, Melchisedec and sanctuary; 1 John has love, abide, God and world.

## Guards against small numbers

Normalization has a weakness that every careful user of it must know. Dividing by a small number magnifies whatever is divided. A section of 446 words in which a single borrowed phrase occurs will post an enormous rate per 1,000 words, because a thousand is more than twice its size; one occurrence is multiplied into more than two. This happened in the atlas when the Asaph psalms were first divided so that Psalm 50 stood alone as a 446-word section, and the per-1,000 echo figures made that one psalm a partner of nearly everything. The cure was partly to let a section be a list of chapters rather than a single run, so that Psalm 50 joins Psalms 73 to 83, and partly to add a warning.

Several such warnings are built into the atlas, and each is a line drawn on size so that normalization cannot mislead unnoticed. A section of a book under a thousand words is marked "few" beside its name, so its rows are read lightly. An obs/exp ratio built on fewer than twenty echoes is printed in brackets and marked "few", because a small book with a handful of shared idioms will always post a high ratio. A focus word on a book page must occur at least five times in the book before its neighbors are reported, since a word that occurs three times has no neighborhood to speak of. A neighbor must be met at least twice to be listed, since one meeting is not neighborhood. And the home of a word must rest on at least five occurrences, so that two uses of a word in Jude do not make Jude its second home.

The general lesson is that a normalized figure should always be read with the size it was divided by in view. The atlas prints the raw count beside the rate wherever it can, so the reader can do exactly that.

## Reading a page with these ideas in hand

It may help to follow a single row of a single page from the text to the number, with every step named.

Open the book page for Malachi, by choosing Book and Malachi in the top bar or by typing `[Malachi]` in the Ask line. Section 1, the signature words, has "hosts" at the top. Behind that row, the following has happened. Every token of the Bible was given a root in the first normalization: its apostrophes straightened, its capitals lowered unless it was a divine name, its Strong's tag placed, or inferred, or absorbed, and failing all of those its English stem taken. Every token whose root is the Hebrew word for "hosts" was then counted in Malachi, giving the weight of 24, and in the rest of the Old Testament, since this is a Hebrew root and is compared within its own testament. Each count was divided by the size of the text it came from and multiplied by a thousand, giving the two rates per 1,000 words shown in the row, which is the second normalization. The two counts and the two sizes were then put through Dunning's G squared: the expected counts worked out, the observed counts set against them, the logarithms taken, summed and doubled, giving the keyness of 126.8, far above the one-in-a-thousand threshold of 10.8. The number of Malachi's chapters that hold the word was counted to give its reach, the keyness was scaled by that share of chapters to give its spread, and the word's keyness in each single chapter was computed to find its depth and its deepest chapter. In notation the whole row can be written `'hosts' [Malachi] (weight 24, keyness 126.8)`. And a single click on the row fills the verse pane with the twenty-four verses that were counted, so that any doubt about the number can be settled by reading the text.

That last point is the most important thing to say about normalization in Word Atlas. Every figure on every page can be traced, in one click, to the verses that produced it. Normalization changes numbers into other numbers, and it is easy for such transformations to hide an error. The atlas's answer is that no number is more than one click from its evidence. When a figure looks odd, the reader clicks it and reads what produced it.

## The limits of the method

Normalization makes comparisons fair only with respect to the thing divided by. It puts a short book and a long book on one scale of size, but it cannot correct for anything else that differs between them. A book of genealogies and a book of poetry differ in more than length, and a normalized rate will not tell the reader which difference is at work. Keyness adds a measure of surprise, but surprise is not meaning: a word can be highly key in a chapter because the chapter is a list (the deepest words in the whole Bible, by the atlas's own count, are "families" in Numbers 26, "suburbs" in Joshua 21 and "begat" in Matthew 1). Rarity rewards unusual words, but an unusual word shared by two passages may be shared by accident.

The first normalization has its own limits. The roots are only as good as the tagging and the rules that fill its gaps, and every rule described above was adjusted at least once after reading its results against the text. On an English build, the roots are only as good as the stemmer's table of exceptions. And because the formulas and the echoes within a testament are now counted on Hebrew and Greek roots, while a Hebrew root and a Greek root never match, the echoes between the testaments are still found by English wording, which rests on the translators' idiom. The pages say so wherever it applies, marking such echoes "by English".

For all these reasons the rules are kept in named settings in two files, `atlas_text.py` for the rules applied when the atlas is built and `atlas_pages.py` for those applied when a page is asked for, and each setting carries a comment recording what it does and what values were tried. A reader who wishes to know what a different choice of divisor, threshold or rule would do can change it, rebuild under a new label, and compare the two builds page by page in the Build box. That is how every rule in this essay was set.

## The vocabulary gathered

It may help to close by gathering the vocabulary this essay has introduced, in the order a reader will meet it on a page.

A scale is the amount of text being looked at, from the whole Bible down to a passage, written in square brackets. A token is one word in one place; its spelling is how the text prints it; its root is what it is counted as. A root is either a Strong's number, named by H for Hebrew or G for Greek, or an English stem where the text has no tag. A tag is the Strong's number the tagged text attaches to a token. A tagged token is placed when its number came from the tagging, inferred when it took the number its spelling usually carries (marked with a tilde), and absorbed when it was folded into a neighboring tagged word by the gloss rule or by its constant company (marked with an equals sign). The lexicon is Strong's dictionary as loaded into the atlas, and a gloss is an English meaning it gives for a number, as against a rendering, which is a word the translators actually used. A stop word is a function word left out of the counts but kept inside phrases, and the stop list is the set of them. A content word is any word that is not a stop word. A stem is the English base of a word with its endings removed, found by the stemmer and its table of exceptions.

Weight is a count at a scale, or, for an echo, the summed rarity of its words. Normalization is the division of a count by the size of what it was counted in, reported per 1,000 words, or by a total, reported as a share. Obs/exp is an observed count set against the count the partner's length alone would predict. Keyness is how surprising a word's count is here compared with the rest of its testament or the Bible, computed by the log-likelihood in Dunning's G squared form, with 3.8, 6.6 and 10.8 marking one chance in twenty, a hundred and a thousand. Rarity is the negative logarithm of a word's share of its testament.

Signature words are the words with the highest keyness at a scale. Reach is how widely a word is spread, spread is keyness scaled by the share of chapters reached, and a local word lives in under a fifth of a book's chapters. Depth is a word's highest keyness in any one chapter, and leading words are the words whose deepest chapter is the one being read. The window is five words either side within a verse; a neighbor is a word met inside the window more often than chance; a tie is one such pairing, its pull the strength of it, and a word's shadow the sum of its ties. A formula is a set phrase of two to five words, counted as a run of units; an echo is a rare formula shared between books; a quotation-grade echo is five or more words found in exactly two verses of the Bible; and kin is a pair of verses sharing three or more rare words in any order. A word's home is the book that prefers it most.

And the notation, one mark to one meaning: double quotes for the exact words of the text, single quotes for a word as the atlas counts it, square brackets for the scale, a minus sign inside them for a scale with a part removed, parentheses for a named measurement, braces for a set in any order, angle brackets for words in a fixed order, a plus sign for two words meeting within the window, a lower-case x for two books set side by side, an arrow for an echo or kin link, and an equals sign for spellings folded into one root.

With those words and those marks, any line in the atlas can be read aloud as a plain English sentence, and every number in it can be followed back, through the normalizations that shaped it, to the verses of the Bible it was counted from.
