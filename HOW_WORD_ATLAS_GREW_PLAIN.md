# How Word Atlas Grew: the easy-to-read version

October 4, 2026. Andrew Hopkins, with Claude.

This is the story of a computer program called Word Atlas, told in the order it happened. It is written for a reader who has never seen the program and who may not know the technical words that the longer account uses. Every technical word is explained the first time it appears. A reader who follows the story will understand what the program does and why it was built the way it was, because the program is the sum of the decisions described here.

## What the program is for

Word Atlas is a program for studying the words of the Bible. It counts how often each word is used, where it is used, and which other words tend to appear near it. It then shows the results as pages of tables, the way an atlas shows maps. Andrew Hopkins, who writes about the Bible and also writes computer programs, designed it with the help of Claude, an artificial intelligence. Andrew already had a program called Bible Search Lite, which can search several English translations of the Bible, and Word Atlas grew out of that work.

## The idea

The program began as a picture in Andrew's mind rather than as a plan for software. He asked us to imagine a map of the world. On a world map you see how the continents relate to one another. If you turn the page to a map of one country, you see a different set of relations. If you turn again to a map of one state, you see a third set that was invisible on the world map. Andrew asked whether words might behave the same way. Some words, he suggested, cast a large shadow over many other words, and some cast almost no shadow at all. The word "Lord" would cast a large shadow. The word "a" would cast a small one. He also suggested that the size of a word's shadow should have something to do with how spread out the word is. A word that is used many times in a small space should count for less than a word that is used the same number of times but is spread over a wide area.

The answer was that the "shadow" is really three different things twisted together, and that separating them is what makes the idea possible to build. The first is plain frequency, which simply means how often a word appears. Frequency on its own would make "the" the most important word in the Bible, which is not useful. The second is what language scientists call dispersion, which means how evenly a word is spread through a text. This was exactly the difference Andrew had noticed between a word clumped in one place and a word spread everywhere. The third, and the one Andrew was really reaching for, is a word's pull on its neighbors. If you record which words appear near "Lord" more often than chance would predict, and then do the same for every word, you get a web of connections. In that web, the size of a word's shadow is how many other words it is tied to and how strongly. This is where "a" finally falls away. It is everywhere, but it attracts nothing in particular.

The part of the idea that mattered most was turning the pages. Computed for the whole Bible, the web is dominated by the words Lord, God, king, Israel, people and son. Turn to the book of Psalms and "Lord" is still huge, but its neighbors have changed to praise, mercy, trust and soul. Turn to John chapter 15 and "vine" is the largest thing on the page, although it is nearly invisible when you look at the whole Bible. Watching the same word's neighborhood change as you move through the text is a real observation about how the writers used that word. That is what the tool would be for: finding the vocabulary that makes each book distinctive, following an important word through the Bible to see where its company changes, and finding groups of words that travel together.

Two warnings were given at the start, and both turned out to be true. First, the measures work far better on roots than on raw spellings. A root is the basic form of a word. The spellings "say", "saith", "said" and "spake" are all forms of one root, and if they are counted separately, one word's shadow is split four ways. Second, the numbers are only as meaningful as the units the text is divided into. A fixed window of a few words inside a verse would be more honest than a whole chapter.

## Where to start

Andrew was asked which scale he would want to read first: the whole Bible, a book, a chapter or a single word. He said probably the word level, but he added that the honest answer depended on where the important patterns were, and that he did not want to miss something through inexperience. He suggested the book of Joel as a first test, because it is a short book with a strong focus and rich vocabulary. He also asked whether n-grams would help. An n-gram is simply a run of words that appear next to each other, such as "the day of the LORD", which is a run of five words.

The recommendation was to design the program around that worry rather than guess. The world map, the country map and the state map are not three separate sets of data. They are three views of one set. If every occurrence of every word in the Bible is stored once, with its book, chapter, verse and position, then "word level" and "book level" are only different ways of asking the same stored facts a question. The choice of scale becomes a view, not a commitment. So the design effort should go into the foundation, and the views should be cheap to make and easy to throw away.

N-grams, it was explained, catch something that the shadow idea by itself misses. A neighbor window asks which words tend to be near each other in any order. An n-gram is a fixed run of adjacent words, and the Bible is built from such runs: "saith the LORD", "and it came to pass", "the day of the LORD". Recording them shows which set phrases a book leans on. More interesting still, when a phrase that is rare in the Bible as a whole turns up in two places, that is a machine-assisted way of finding quotation, which is one writer repeating another on purpose. Joel was a good test for both ideas. It is short enough to check every result against the text, and it is dense with both kinds of pattern, from "the day of the LORD" five times in three chapters to the words that Peter quotes from Joel in Acts chapter 2.

The first experiment was therefore sketched as a small script that would produce four plain-text reports for one book. A script is a short computer program that does one job. The four reports were: the words far more common in the book than elsewhere, the phrases that were likewise distinctive, the neighbors of a chosen word inside the book set beside its neighbors across the whole Bible, and every other place in the Bible where the book's distinctive phrases occur.

## The plan

Andrew agreed to start with the King James Version, which is the English translation of 1611 usually called the KJV. He asked whether other English versions should come in too, whether a different interface and some graphics would help, and for a fixed vocabulary to describe the results, since a document would eventually have to explain the system in plain terms. He asked for a plan, and a plan document was written that day.

The plan made these decisions. The KJV alone at first, but with room in the tables for other translations later. The KJV in Andrew's database carried Strong's numbers, and that mattered a great deal. Strong's numbers are a system, made in the nineteenth century by James Strong, that gives every Hebrew word of the Old Testament and every Greek word of the New Testament its own number. A reader who does not know Hebrew or Greek can still see, through the number, which original word lies behind an English word. Having those numbers in the database meant that the whole project could later be repeated on the Hebrew and Greek roots, where the shadows would be truest.

The plan also fixed a vocabulary of plain words, each with its technical name given only for looking things up. The main ones are these. The scale is the stretch of text being looked at: the Bible, a book, a chapter or a passage. The weight of a word is how many times it occurs. The reach of a word is how many chapters it occurs in. The pull between two words is how much more often they appear near each other than chance would predict. A word's shadow is the sum of its pull on all its neighbors. The signature words of a book are the words far more common in it than elsewhere. A formula is a set phrase of two to five words. An echo is a formula found in two different books. The window is the stretch of five words on either side of a word within which other words count as its neighbors. And a root is the basic form of a word, with all its spellings folded together.

The plan set out six phases: the Joel experiment; the foundation database; a text atlas made of tables only; the pictures; more texts, meaning the Strong's roots and other translations; and finally the guide, to be written last from the working program. The program would be a separate window in the same plain style as Bible Search Lite, with comments throughout the code. Word Atlas was accepted as the name.

## Phase 1: the Joel experiment

The Bible database was too large to copy into the workspace where Claude works, so the script was tested against a public-domain copy of the KJV loaded into the same table layout. There was one known difference. That copy printed the divine name as "Lord", where Andrew's copy prints it as "LORD" in capital letters, which is the KJV's way of showing the Hebrew name of God.

Even on the test text the four reports were encouraging. Joel's signature words came out as cankerworm, zion, pasture, wine, palmerworm, locust, desolate, heathen, valley, howl, wither and rain, which is the book in a dozen words. Its signature formulas included "the day of the LORD", "the pastures of the wilderness" and "the valley of decision". The echo report found that Joel 1:2 and Malachi 3:7 share the phrase "the days of your fathers". The weakest report on a book that short was the neighbors, because a word that occurs only thirteen times has very little neighborhood to rank.

Andrew ran the script on his own database and wrote a reading of both Joel and Malachi. Malachi's character came through almost perfectly. The word "hosts" stood far above every other word. The phrase "saith the LORD of hosts" appeared in 21 of the book's 55 verses. And the program found, without being told to look, a pattern that scholars call the disputation: the phrase "ye say, Wherein?" appears five times in Malachi and nowhere else in the Bible. Joel read as a different kind of book, full of concrete nouns from the natural world, and the neighbors of the word "day" told the whole story. Inside Joel, "day" keeps company with gloominess and darkness. Across the Bible, it keeps company with month, sabbath, seventh and feast. The echoes report also rediscovered, from nothing but shared runs of words, the passage that Peter quotes from Joel in Acts chapter 2.

His reading also listed what was wrong, and each point became a rule. For example, the formula table listed three overlapping pieces of one phrase from Malachi 1:11, so overlapping pieces are now grown back into one phrase and shown once. A stray letter "s" in the echoes turned out to come from a curly apostrophe in the database that was splitting the word "Esau's", so apostrophes are now straightened before words are split. And the common fragments of "saith the LORD" cluttered Malachi's echoes, so an echo is now judged after setting such prophetic voice tags aside, and it must still contain two meaningful words.

## Phase 2: the atlas database

With the four reports proven, Andrew asked for the next step. It was the foundation: do the computation once for the whole KJV and store it, so that any page at any scale becomes a quick question to the stored results. A database is a file of tables that a program can search very quickly. A build script reads the KJV and fills a database file called atlas.db with every verse, every word and its root, the weight and reach of every word at the Bible, book and chapter scales, the pull between word pairs, every formula of two to five words found in two or more verses, and the echoes, computed once over the whole Bible. The build took under twenty seconds. A query script then answered any page in about half a second: a book page, a chapter page, and a word page that shows one row for each of the 66 books, a picture that was later called the shadow map.

Andrew read Ezekiel and Revelation on the new pages and sent a long review. Ezekiel showed two vocabularies under one cover: the architecture and measurements of chapters 40 to 48 rose to the top of the whole book's list while appearing in only seven of its 48 chapters, and the judgment vocabulary was spread across the book. The formula table recovered the skeleton of Ezekiel's prose, such as "saith the Lord GOD" in 199 verses. Revelation's echoes were exactly what the commentaries say the book is built on, with Daniel supplying the most.

He asked for six changes, and all six were made. One was a new column called spread, which is keyness scaled by the share of chapters a word reaches, together with a mark called "local" for words that live in under a fifth of a book's chapters; Ezekiel's cubits, chambers and arches are marked local, a book within the book. Keyness is a technical word that will come up often, so it should be explained here. Keyness is a statistical measure of how much more often a word appears in one text than you would expect from the rest of the Bible. A high keyness means the word is far more common here than elsewhere. A keyness near zero means the word is used at about the same rate as everywhere else.

The sixth change was the deepest. The relationship between Ezekiel and Revelation was nearly invisible in the echoes, because John, the writer of Revelation, paraphrases rather than quotes. Ezekiel 47 and Revelation 22 share the words river, tree, fruit, leaves and healing, but they share no exact phrase. Andrew suggested comparing sets of rare words instead of runs of words. The version that worked tests one verse against another: two verses are kin when they share three or more rare words. With that rule, Revelation 22:2 came up for Ezekiel 47 in the company of Psalm 1:3 and Jeremiah 17:8, all real tree-and-river relatives, and kin became a new kind of page.

## Two ideas from the reader

Andrew then asked whether words found in the same order in two different books should carry more weight than words shared in any order. The answer was yes, as a bonus rather than a requirement. Order is a strong sign of borrowing, because a writer who has another text in mind tends to walk through it in the same sequence even while changing every phrase. But translation and paraphrase scramble order, so a strict rule would have ranked the famous Ezekiel and Revelation pair too low. The measure chosen was the longest run of shared words that keeps the same order in both verses, and the kin page gained an "in order" column.

He raised a second idea with some hesitation, calling it possibly meaningless. The plan valued a word spread over a large area above a word repeated in close proximity. Could the repeated-in-proximity case have a different kind of value, vertical as opposed to horizontal? The reply was that this has a name in biblical scholarship. The scholars Martin Buber and Franz Rosenzweig called it the Leitwort, a German word meaning "leading word": a word deliberately repeated within a short stretch so that the repetition carries the meaning. Reach and depth are two separate things a word can have. Reach tells you what a book is about. Depth tells you where a passage is pressing. "Fruit" six times in Ezekiel 47:12 is enormous depth. The idea became a chart, with reach along the bottom and depth up the side, one dot per word for a book.

## Phase 3: the window

Andrew asked whether it was too early for a graphical interface, which means a program with windows, buttons and tables rather than plain text on a command line. It was not too early. One piece of preparation came first. The page code had been building lines of text directly, so it was split in two. A pages module now builds every page as data, a report made of sections, each section a table of columns and rows that also carries the verse references behind every row. The command-line script became a thin layer that turns that data into text, and the window shows the same data as tables. One set of logic, two displays.

The window borrowed its look from Bible Search Lite. The top bar chooses the kind of page, the book, the chapter and the word. The middle is the page, one table per section. The bottom is the verse pane, and the rule for it was that a number should never be more than one click from the text that produced it. Click any row and the verses behind it appear. Double-click a word to turn to its word page.

Running the window exposed some practical things. PyQt6, the toolkit that draws the windows, was not installed for the system's Python, so Word Atlas got its own virtual environment, which is a private copy of Python with its own packages. And Andrew spotted a mismatch: a neighbor row said "night" met "day" five times, and the pane showed seven verses. The table was counting meetings inside the five-word window, while the pane was showing every verse holding both words anywhere. The pane now applies the same window rule and states both numbers. A Save as text button and a Rebuild atlas button followed.

## The tallies

Andrew's second reading of Ezekiel and Revelation asked for three more changes. The echo list showed only sixty of over two thousand candidate echoes for Ezekiel, and the ranking favored numbers and measurements. A tally by partner book over all the candidates, he said, would tell more about a book's sources than any sixty phrases, and a tally by chapter would separate the temple vision's borrowings from the rest.

The tallies became sections 4a and 4b of the book page. He called them the biggest step forward since the first Joel report. The partner table says what Ezekiel is made of: Jeremiah leads by raw count, two prophets writing in the same decades about the same siege, but measured per thousand words Leviticus is denser, and Zechariah, a later book that borrowed from Ezekiel, jumps to the top. The chapter table draws the structure of Ezekiel in sources: chapters 40 to 42 echo Exodus, Chronicles and Kings, the architecture; chapters 43 to 47 echo Numbers, Leviticus and Exodus, the sacrifices and the priesthood; chapter 48 echoes Numbers and Joshua, the division of the land. So the temple vision is three blocks, each with its own source.

Three requests from that reading were met in one pass. An observed-against-expected column was added, which compares the number of echoes with the number that the partner book's length alone would predict. Each echo was given a weight, the summed rarity of its words, so that bland phrases could not outvote quotations. And a table of the dates when each book was written, approximate and open to editing, was added, so that the partner table can say whether each partner is earlier, contemporary or later. That is the difference between what a prophet read and who read the prophet.

## Phase 4 begins

Andrew asked whether the pictures could be tried, and the first one was the one he had pointed at: chapters down the side, partner books across the top, and the density of echoes shown as color. The echo map became part of every book page. Each cell is shaded by the weight of the echoes between that chapter and that book. Hovering shows the number, and clicking puts the verses from both sides in the pane. On Revelation the map reads as his notes predicted: Daniel darkest in chapters 5, 13 and 20, Exodus in chapters 8 and 9, Ezekiel in chapter 18. The shadow map on the word page was drawn as bars at the same time, one bar per book in Bible order.

## Speaking about it

Andrew then turned to how the program is described. He proposed a set of marks, such as quotation marks, brackets and plus signs, each with a fixed meaning, so that a newcomer could read the reports easily. The notation was kept small, with one meaning per mark. Double quotes mean a formula or echo in the exact words of the text. Single quotes mean a word as the atlas counts it, with all its spellings folded together. Square brackets give the scale, so [Joel] means "within the book of Joel". Parentheses give a named measurement. A plus sign means two words meeting inside the window. A finding then fits on one line: 'day' + 'gloominess' [Joel] (pull 14.1). The notation went into the section titles and into an ask line in the window, where any such line can be typed to produce the page it names.

## Trial and error

Anticipating a phase in which the rules would be changed and the results compared, Andrew asked for a button that recalculates the tables from the window, and added that one might want to go back to an earlier state. The Rebuild atlas button runs the build as a separate process while the window stays usable. Kept builds handle going back: the rebuild asks for a label, and a labeled build is stored with a copy of the rules that made it, so two sets of rules can be compared page by page. Every page names the build it came from.

## Where it stood after the first week

In about a week, Word Atlas went from a map analogy to a program with five files and four kinds of page: book, chapter, word and kin. Every measure in it had been changed at least once by reading its output against the text. That is the method the plan set out, and it is the reason the tool finds things its authors did not put in, such as Ezekiel's temple vision as three blocks with three sources and Malachi's disputations.

## Phase 5: the Hebrew and Greek behind the English

The first week's atlas counted English words. The plan had always said that an English word is the wrong thing to count when the text was written in Hebrew and Greek, and phase 5 fixed that. Andrew's database already carried the KJV with Strong's numbers on its words, and the build was taught to use the numbers as the roots. From then on, H3068, the Hebrew name of God usually printed LORD, and H136, the Hebrew title Adonai usually printed Lord, were counted as two different words whatever the English prints, and "day" in a Gospel was counted as the Greek word G2250 rather than the Hebrew word H3117.

The tagging is not complete, which means that some words in the KJV have no Strong's number attached, and those untagged words became a study of their own. A word the tagger left bare is first placed beside its neighbor's meaning. For example, an untagged "rising" next to H7925 "early" is the same Hebrew word, so it is absorbed into it. Then a word is placed by the company it keeps, and then its number is inferred from its spelling. Each rule reports a count. A round of work on the rules brought the untagged content words down from twelve percent to 3.4 percent, and every report has since carried a line saying which rules made its numbers. The Strong's dictionary, which gives the meaning of each numbered word, became part of the program, and a section called 1a now shows, under every signature word, the Hebrew or Greek word behind it with its KJV meanings and spellings.

Two consequences followed that nobody had asked for. A Hebrew word never occurs in the Greek New Testament, so comparing a word with the whole Bible would make every Greek word look special. The comparison became the word's own testament instead, meaning the Old Testament for a Hebrew word and the New Testament for a Greek one. And the echoes, which had matched English wording, now matched roots, which meant they could no longer cross between the testaments at all. The English bridge was built for that: an echo found across the testaments by its English wording, marked "by English", so that Revelation's debt to Ezekiel stayed visible.

## The book against itself, and against another book

The pages multiplied by reading. The book of Exodus gave a section in which a book is compared with itself, so that chapters sharing rare phrases with other chapters of the same book can be seen, and from it came a table of refrains, which are phrases a book repeats. The Gospels of Matthew and Mark gave the Compare page, which sets two books chapter against chapter and shows where one book follows the other's sequence. From that came the sharing table, which sorts the verses of Mark the way scholars of the Gospels do: 262 verses with a parallel in both Matthew and Luke, 174 in Matthew only, 61 in Luke only, and 181 in neither.

The dossier gathered everything about one book into one file: the book page, its Compare pages, every chapter page and the word pages of its top words. The dossier became the unit of review, meaning the thing a reader reads from start to finish and sends comments on.

The section layer came from Jeremiah and the Psalms, books whose parts scholars had long since mapped. A table in the program lists a book's divisions as lists of chapters, and section 7 reports each part's size and its leading words against the rest of the book, its echo partners, and the parts measured against each other. A book may carry several divisions, and the second is where the source analyses live, meaning the theories of which parts of a book came from which earlier source. Dates went in beside them, a conventional date and a critical date for every book, so that a book whose date scholars dispute says so.

## The manual and the catalogue

By then there were three documents, and a reader could not tell which to open. Andrew asked for one, and the three became the Word Atlas Manual, written in full sentences: how to start, how to read every table on every page, what the measures mean, worked examples book by book, the rules and how to change them, and a lab of twelve commands a new reader can type. A separate file called IMPROVEMENTS.md became the one list of what is proposed and waiting.

The catalogue is the other half of the record. It is a second database, metadata.db, that holds what the program cannot compute from the text itself: the sixty-six books with their testament, their genre and their baseline group, which is the kind of book each one is (Law, History, Poetry, Prophecy, New Testament Narrative, or Epistles, which are the letters of the New Testament); the named passages; the Septuagint's own list of books; and other reference tables. The Septuagint is the ancient Greek translation of the Old Testament, which the writers of the New Testament often quoted. The catalogue is kept in the project's public repository as a text file, and every change refreshes that file.

## The reviewer

From the review of Numbers onward, the work settled into a loop that has not changed since. A second reading, done by Andrew or by a helper he set up, takes a book's dossier and reads it against the text and against what scholars have written, and sends back what the tables got right, what they got wrong, and what a commentary would have expected to see. The fixes are made and tested, and the files are written into Andrew's project folder. Then the next book follows. In this way Numbers got its places and its sources, Deuteronomy its five addresses, Joshua its land register, Judges its framework, Job its eight parts, and on the New Testament side John, Acts, Romans, the Corinthian letters and the rest each got their parts.

The reviewer's expectations were often set before the tables ran, and some held and some did not. The ones that did not hold taught the most. For example, 1 Corinthians 15 was expected to pair with 1 Thessalonians 4, because both chapters are about the resurrection of the dead. Instead it paired with 1 Thessalonians 1 and 2. The reason was that the KJV uses the words "sleep" and "rise" in 1 Thessalonians 4 where 1 Corinthians 15 uses "dead" and "raised", and the echo layer sees wording.

## The kind

Section 1 of a book page had always measured a book's words against the rest of its testament. But against the whole Old Testament, a prophet's list is full of words that are merely common in prophecy. Section 1b measures them against the book's own kind instead, the baseline group that the catalogue gives it, with the book itself left out of the comparison. Measured against the other prophets, Isaiah's leading words become redeemer, salvation, righteousness, created, chosen and remnant, and the words woe, isles and hosts fall away because every prophet uses them.

Getting that table honest took a dozen versions, each from a review. Revelation, whose kind is the Hebrew prophets, came out as a plain frequency list, because a Greek word never occurs in a Hebrew book, so the table learned to compare only with books of the same testament and, when none remain, to print its heading with the reason in place of the rows. The small prophets showed that the table padded. It had been filling to twenty-five rows even on a book of 1,300 words, so that Habakkuk's last rows were words used at exactly the same rate as everywhere else. The table now stops when the keyness falls below a fixed floor. The floor on how many times a word must occur before it is counted now scales with the size of the book, so that Nahum's cankerworm, prey and lion, three or four uses each, are measured at all. And when the whole Bible was read through on the finished tables, the Psalms produced a famous scholarly observation without being asked. Scholars have long noticed that the second group of Psalms, chapters 42 to 72, prefers the Hebrew word Elohim for God where the first group prefers the divine name. The table shows it: the leading word of Book II is Elohim, and the leading word of Book I is the divine name.

## The words no subject drives

A review of 2 Corinthians raised the question that the vocabulary tables cannot answer. Scholars have long argued about whether chapters 10 to 13 of that letter were written separately from chapters 1 to 9. The two parts share almost no rare phrases, but neither does the section about the collection of money in chapters 8 and 9 share any with its neighbors, and nobody thinks that section was a separate letter. A block of text that talks about something else shares little with its neighbors whether or not the same person wrote it. What would separate a change of subject from a change of writer is the vocabulary that no subject drives: the small words such as "and", "but", "for", "in", "he" and "you", which a writer reaches for without choosing them. Every writer uses them at their own rates, and the rates stay put when the subject changes.

The method for comparing those rates is called Burrows' Delta, after the scholar John Burrows, who introduced it in 2002. It works like this. For each small word, measure how often it appears in a text, per thousand words. Compare that rate with the average rate across many texts, and express the difference as a number of standard deviations, which is a standard way of saying how unusual the rate is. Then the Delta between two texts is the average of the differences between their numbers over all the small words. A Delta of zero would mean two texts use the small words at exactly the same rates. The program already had Burrows' Delta in a side script, and the KJV's Strong's tagging carries numbers on the Greek small words, so a function-word layer was built for the New Testament in an afternoon. A function word is a grammar word with little meaning of its own, as opposed to a content word such as "mountain" or "sacrifice". The layer measures twenty-three Greek function words and produces two tables: one that sets a book beside each book of its kind, and one that sets the parts of a book against the rest of it.

A Delta number means nothing on its own, so the tables had to be calibrated, which means giving the reader something to compare the number with, and the reviewer did the calibrating one step at a time. First came two yardsticks: the typical Delta between two different books, and the typical Delta between the two halves of one book. Then yardsticks by size, since Delta rises as a text shrinks, with the typical figure and the figure that nine in ten same-book runs fall under. Then the same yardsticks struck within the book's own kind, since a letter changes its register inside itself, meaning its tone and manner, in a way that a Gospel narrative does not. Then a second Delta that leaves out the pronouns, meaning words like "I", "we" and "you", since a letter written in self-defense is in the first person for reasons of subject rather than authorship.

With all that in place, the two halves of 2 Corinthians sit at the edge of the normal range for a letter and not beyond it. Colossians' nearest neighbor is Ephesians at exactly the figure for two halves of one book, which matches what scholars have long said about those two letters. And an old proposal about the letter to the Hebrews was tested. The scholar William Wrede argued in 1906 that chapter 13 of Hebrews was added later to turn a sermon into a letter. The function words show that chapter 13 is as much like the rest of Hebrews in its habits as any chapter of a letter is like its own letter. What changes in chapter 13 is only the pronouns, because it is the one place where the writer finally says "you" to the readers. That is a change of form with no change of hand, which was in fact Wrede's own position.

## How wide the vocabulary is

Hebrews was also the test case for a measure that the function words cannot see. Hebrews has about 150 words that are found nowhere else in the New Testament, which is what scholars of Greek style have long meant when they called its author a man of letters. A table called 1d measures this. It counts hapax legomena, a Greek term that means words that occur only once in the whole testament, and it counts a book's own roots, meaning words found in no other book, and it gives both as rates per thousand words, beside each book of the kind in order of size.

Every such rate depends on the size of the text, so beside each rate stands what a run of chapters of the same size, cut from the other books of the testament, typically gives. That comparison settled two old arguments at once. Hebrews' gap from Romans is not a matter of size. And an argument made by P. N. Harrison in 1921, that the three letters called the Pastorals have far more rare words per page than Paul's other letters, survives the objection his critics raised about size, because the rate of once-used words barely moves with the size of a text while other measures collapse. At the other end, 1 John has the poorest vocabulary in the New Testament by a wide margin: half the expected number of different words and a twelfth of the expected number of once-used words, which is the number for a style that circles a small set of words.

## The whole Bible read through

With the New Testament reviews done, the sixty-six dossiers were regenerated and read as a set. No page crashed, no number was missing, and every table that could not measure something said why. Four small things came out of the reading and were fixed: a stray "None", which is the word the Python language prints when it has nothing, in some shadow maps; a lone heading printing above a table that had declined to measure; phrase rows printed twice when two different keys produced the same display text; and a weakness in the size comparisons of table 1d. One old cost was also found by timing each page section by section. A test that decides whether a word is a proper name had been scanning all 31,000 verses of the Bible twice for every signature word of every chapter page. It is now done once for the whole text. The repository, which is the public copy of the program on the website GitHub, went public the same week, with the catalogue included as a text file and the large database files left out.

## Two tagged texts

The list had said for a week that work on the Septuagint was on hold until a Greek New Testament tagged with Strong's numbers could be brought in. The reason was that the two taggings, the KJV's and the Septuagint's, sometimes use different numbers for the same Greek word, and those differences could only be guessed from how often each number appeared. The text that solved the problem was the TAGNT, the Translators Amalgamated Greek New Testament, published by STEPBible, a project of Tyndale House in Cambridge, under a license that allows free use with credit. It holds every word of every major edition of the Greek New Testament in one file, including the Textus Receptus, which is the Greek text the King James translators worked from, with each word marked by the editions that carry it, parsed and tagged with its Strong's number. It went into the program's Greek database in two seconds, and the differences between the two taggings were then read off the text instead of guessed: lay the KJV's numbers and the Greek text's numbers side by side verse by verse, and where they differ by one number on each side, the pair is a difference of tagging. Forty-seven such pairs recur, and eleven of them went into the catalogue's table of equivalents, which now has sixty-two rows.

The Hebrew followed the next day by the same pattern. The TAHOT, the Translators Amalgamated Hebrew Old Testament, from the same source, tags every element of a Hebrew word. That matters because Hebrew attaches many of its small words to the front or the back of other words. The word for "and" is a single letter attached to the front of the next word, and so is the word for "the", and so are "in", "to", "like" and "from"; the words for "my", "your", "his" and "their" are endings attached to the back. The KJV's tagging gives these attached words no numbers at all, so the function-word layer could not be measured on the Old Testament. The TAHOT gives each of them a number of its own, and with it the layer now measures every Old Testament book on twenty-seven Hebrew features. Two forms of the letter for "and" are kept apart on purpose, because one of them is the form used to carry a story forward from one event to the next, and its rate against the plain "and" is the mark of narrative. Jonah, which is a story, has that narrative form at 80 per thousand elements; Lamentations, which is poetry, has it at 13.

The first result on the Hebrew side was the one the layer was built for. Scholars have argued for two centuries that Isaiah chapters 40 to 66 were written later than chapters 1 to 39. The two halves are 0.94 apart in the Delta measure when the pronouns are included, beyond the normal range for two parts of one book, and 0.69 apart without the pronouns, which is within it. Once the "I am the LORD" and the "thou" of the second half's words of comfort are set aside, the two halves differ in their habits by about as much as two halves of any one prophetic book differ. What stands above the line is the pronouns, which is to say the mode of address rather than the hand. The table does not settle the argument, but it says exactly which part of the difference is which.

## Where it stands now

Word Atlas is at version 0.10.55. It has nine kinds of page and a dossier that holds them. A book page now has four ways of setting a book against its kind: which words it owns, whose habits it has, how wide its vocabulary is, and how its parts behave. Three original-language texts, the Septuagint, the Greek New Testament and the Hebrew Old Testament, sit in one database beside the KJV. There is a catalogue of what the text cannot say about itself, a manual of some two hundred pages with a worked example for most books, and a public repository that anyone can read. Every table on every page has been read against the text by a second reader, and the standard that reading set is the one the program is held to. A table should say only what a reader can defend. It should print the reason when it cannot measure something. And it should tell the reader, in its own footer, how far to trust each number.

What comes next is on the list: pages for the Septuagint, now that the equivalents between the taggings are as complete as the two taggings allow; a way to define a section that crosses from one book into another, for stories such as the reign of David that run from 2 Samuel into 1 Kings; a layer for persons, places and times; a test script; and a small language for defining new measures without writing Python. This account tells how the program was reached. The manual tells what it is.
